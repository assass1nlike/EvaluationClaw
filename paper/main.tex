\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Frontier large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable capabilities
across a wide range of tasks, from scientific reasoning and software engineering
to clinical decision support and legal analysis~\citep{}. 
As these models continue to improve and are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, it becomes essential to accurately assess their 能力好坏
Benchmarks are the primary means by which the community measures model capabilities, compares different systems, and tracks progress over time, and they play a central role in guiding both model development and deployment decisions. 
Consequently, building reliable and comprehensive benchmarks for evaluating LLM capabilities has become an important problem in its own right.

Despite their central role, most of the benchmarks currently used to evaluate LLMs are constructed by human craft. Building such a benchmark requires domain
experts to design tasks, collect data, write reference solutions, and verify correctness. These requirements make human construction both expensive
and slow, so that a benchmark typically takes a long time to produce and is
already at risk of obsolescence by the time it is released. More fundamentally,
the static nature of handcrafted benchmarks means that their contents are fixed
at release: as models improve, performance on a fixed set of items rises toward
the ceiling, and the benchmark gradually loses its ability to distinguish
between models. Given the rapid pace at which model capabilities advance, a
manually constructed benchmark can remain informative for only a short period,
after which it ceases to provide a meaningful signal for comparing models or for
guiding further improvement.

Motivated by these limitations, a growing line of work has proposed automated
benchmark construction frameworks. Rather than relying on costly human authoring,
these methods use LLMs to generate benchmark items directly: seed examples or
domain documents are used to prompt a model to synthesize new questions together
with their answers, after which the generated items are filtered, deduplicated,
or otherwise validated to form a benchmark. Such frameworks substantially reduce
the cost and turnaround time of benchmark construction and have made it possible
to produce evaluation sets at a scale that manual authoring cannot match.

However, existing frameworks remain limited in ways that keep them from being
widely adopted in practice. First, because they generate items either from
pre-existing datasets or entirely from the model's own knowledge, they are
confined to what is already represented in that data or knowledge; evaluation
demands that fall outside this distribution---for instance, when the target
capability is sparsely covered by available data or lies beyond what current
models have internalized---are difficult to align with. Second, these frameworks
almost exclusively target static question formats, such as multiple-choice or
short-generation items, and do not support the interactive, environment-grounded
tasks that are now a central focus of frontier LLM capabilities. Third, the few
frameworks that do support iterative refinement rely on simple proxy metrics to
guide iteration, such as error rate or discriminative power; optimizing such
proxies does not reveal which capabilities a model is actually weak at, and can
even degrade item quality, as items are made harder or more ambiguous without
becoming more informative. As a result, automated benchmark construction has yet
to be broadly adopted, and benchmark development remains largely manual.

\section{Related Works}
\subsection{Human-Constructed Benchmarks}
Benchmarking is the primary instrument for measuring progress in language model development, and the dominant methodology remains expert authoring. Starting from shared natural language understanding suites~\citep{wang2018glue}, human-written
benchmarks have scaled along breadth~\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu,bigbench2023},
difficulty~\citep{rein2024gpqa,phang2025hle}, and increasingly toward agentic competence, where the unit of evaluation is a multi-step trajectory rather than a single response~\citep{jimenez2024swebench,xie2024osworld,mialon2024gaia}.
More recent efforts curate items adversarially~\citep{kiela2021dynabench} or
periodically regenerate them from newly published material
\citep{white2024livebench,jain2024livecodebench}. 

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, these costs are not merely logistical:
expert authoring is expensive and slow, and because a published benchmark is a
fixed object, its items saturate and are progressively compromised by
contamination as models improve. The refresh mechanisms
above mitigate this without removing it, since their rate remains bounded by human effort while model capabilities advance unboundedly---and contamination can be present in an item set from the moment it is built, as when an instance exposes the information needed to
solve it, a failure mode that survives filtering because each item looks
well-formed in isolation~\citep{balloccu2024leakcheatrepeat}.

\subsection{Automated Benchmark Construction Frameworks}

A growing line of work replaces expert authoring with LLM-based generation, in
which seed examples or domain documents prompt a model to synthesize items
together with their answers, after which the outputs are filtered,
deduplicated, or otherwise validated
\citep{wang2023selfinstruct,xu2023evolinstruct,bai2023lmexam}. More recent
frameworks cast construction itself as an optimization problem, searching for
item populations that attain properties specified at the outset such as
difficulty or discriminative power~\citep{li2025autobencher}. These frameworks
substantially reduce
construction cost, but they inherit three limitations. First, because generation
is conditioned on pre-existing datasets or on the model's own parametric
knowledge, the resulting items are confined to what that conditioning data
already covers, and evaluation demands outside that distribution---capabilities
sparsely represented in available corpora, or beyond what current models have
internalized---are difficult to target. Second, they predominantly produce static
item formats such as multiple-choice or short-generation questions, and do not
instantiate the interactive, environment-grounded tasks that now carry much of
the frontier capability signal. Third, the frameworks that do support iterative
refinement drive it with simple proxy objectives, such as error rate or
discriminative power; optimizing these proxies does not identify which
capabilities a model actually lacks, and can degrade item quality by making items
harder or more ambiguous without making them more informative. Nor do these
objectives detect the opposite failure, in which an item is well-formed and
apparently hard yet admits a shortcut that satisfies it without exercising the
targeted capability---a failure documented even in widely used human-authored
item sets and their curated revisions
\citep{swebenchverified2024,openai2026swebenchverified}---since a proxy
measured over pass rates cannot distinguish a correct solution from one that
bypasses the intent of the item. Together, these
limitations explain why automated construction has yet to displace manual
authoring in practice. The design of \textsc{EvalClaw} is organized around
them.
\section{EvalClaw Design}\label{sec:design}

\textsc{EvalClaw} addresses the three limitations identified in Section~\ref{sec:intro}
through three co-designed components: a \emph{Research-assisted Planner} that grounds
item specifications in external resources, an \emph{EvalClaw-Harness} that decomposes
construction into manageable, tool-supported subtasks, and a
\emph{hypothesis-driven Analyzer} that replaces proxy-metric iteration with
targeted capability diagnosis. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives an overview of
the full pipeline. We describe each component, its motivation, and the ablation
conditions used to isolate its contribution.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Research-assisted Planner}
\label{sec:planner}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Limitation addressed.}
Existing automated frameworks generate items by either transforming a fixed seed
corpus---restricting coverage to what that corpus already contains---or prompting
a model from parametric knowledge alone, which caps difficulty at what the model
has already internalized and inflates cost when the intended capability lies
outside that knowledge. Neither path can reliably reach evaluation demands that
are sparsely covered by available data or that require authoritative external
material (domain specifications, updated standards, real-world artefacts) to
ground the items correctly.

\paragraph{Design.}
The Planner begins from a high-level capability specification and, before
committing to an item plan, enters a \emph{Research loop}.  In each iteration of
the loop the Planner emits a focused natural-language query; a retrieval backend
returns a summary of relevant material together with source URLs.  The Planner
uses these results to identify which external resources should inform each part
of the planned item---reference documentation, domain datasets, real artefacts,
or authoritative specifications---and records explicit resource assignments in
the item plan it passes downstream.  The loop terminates when the Planner judges
that the plan is sufficiently grounded; the number of iterations is not fixed in
advance.  This design separates the question of \emph{what to evaluate} (the
capability specification) from the question of \emph{what material should ground
the evaluation} (the retrieved resources), and allows the latter to be
determined adaptively rather than by whichever seed corpus happened to be
available at construction time. The ablation conditions used to isolate the
Research loop's contribution are described in Section~\ref{sec:settings}.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{EvalClaw-Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Limitation addressed.}
Existing frameworks commit to a fixed generation paradigm---transforming
documents into questions, mutating code repositories to produce repair tasks,
sampling tool-call chains and inverting them into instructions, or simulating
multi-agent dialogues---and the set of item types each framework can produce is
therefore determined by its paradigm. Wrapping such a paradigm in an outer loop
or evolutionary operator replicates the paradigm's structure rather than
transcending it. For complex agent tasks in particular, prior work typically
designs a bespoke construction procedure for each target domain; tasks that
require interaction with other agents, multi-modal environments, or
programmatically verified state are supported only in isolated, domain-specific
systems.

\paragraph{Design.}
Rather than fixing a generation paradigm, \textsc{EvalClaw} represents every
benchmark item in a unified, schema-complete \emph{EvalClaw-TaskDefinition}
(JSON). A TaskDefinition can express, in a single object: the natural-language
task instruction; any number of participant agents and their role specifications;
the environment configuration required to instantiate the task (Docker images,
filesystem state, network topology, database schemas); synthetic data to be
generated and injected; and the programmatic evaluator used to judge completion.
This representation makes no assumption about which of these fields are populated:
a simple multiple-choice item, a multi-turn tool-use trajectory, and a
multi-agent collaborative task with a live execution environment are all
instances of the same schema.

Filling a TaskDefinition from scratch is a large and structurally heterogeneous
task, and delegating it directly to an LLM produces high failure rates when the
item is complex. The \emph{EvalClaw-Harness} manages this by decomposing the
construction into a set of parallel subtasks, each scoped to one component of
the TaskDefinition. Given the Planner's item plan, the Builder instantiates the
Harness, which fans out into separate construction threads for environment
configuration, role-agent specification, data synthesis, and evaluator
definition. Each thread is equipped with purpose-specific tools (container
management utilities, schema validators, code execution sandboxes, etc.) and
operates independently. Once all threads complete, a \emph{Quality Check} stage
runs two verification passes: a structural check that validates the assembled
TaskDefinition against the full schema, and a subagent review that instantiates
the environment and executes the evaluator against a reference solution to
confirm that the item is well-posed. Items that fail either check are returned to
the relevant construction thread for repair before advancing to the evaluation
pipeline.

The ablation conditions used to isolate the Harness's contribution are
described in Section~\ref{sec:settings}.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hypothesis-driven Analyzer}
\label{sec:analyzer}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Limitation addressed.}
Frameworks that support iterative refinement drive iteration toward one of two
proxy objectives: maximising the aggregate failure rate of a target model, or
maximising the performance gap between strong and weak models.  Both objectives
treat success and failure as undifferentiated scalar signals---they record
\emph{that} a model failed, but not \emph{which capability} the failure
implicates.  Iterating on such signals produces items that are harder or more
discriminative in aggregate, but the items generated in successive rounds are not
systematically related to one another: failures arising from a common underlying
weakness accumulate only by chance.  The resulting item pool is therefore
scattered across capability dimensions, and the benchmark provides no structured
account of what a model actually lacks or how it might improve.

\paragraph{Design.}
The \emph{Analyzer} operates after a target model has been evaluated on an
existing item pool.  Rather than extracting a scalar difficulty signal, the
Analyzer performs a \emph{hypothesis-driven} diagnosis.  It jointly examines
item content and model responses, forms a natural-language hypothesis about a
specific capability deficit---for instance, that the model fails to propagate
constraints across tool-call boundaries, or that it incorrectly assumes sorted
input when none is guaranteed---and then \emph{invokes the full construction
pipeline} with that hypothesis as the capability specification.  The pipeline
produces a small, targeted set of items whose design is derived from the
hypothesis.  The model is evaluated on this set, and the results either support
the hypothesis (the model fails on the targeted items at a rate consistent with
the hypothesised deficit) or refute it (the model succeeds, indicating that the
original failures had a different cause).  A refuted hypothesis is revised and
the cycle repeats; a supported hypothesis is recorded as a confirmed capability
gap, and the Analyzer is encouraged to extend the diagnosis to related capability
dimensions before moving to a new hypothesis.

This loop differs structurally from proxy-metric iteration in two respects.
First, the items produced in each round are functionally related---they are all
designed to probe the same hypothesised deficit---so the resulting item pool is
interpretable as a structured diagnostic rather than a collection of hard
instances.  Second, the stopping criterion is evidential rather than
metric-driven: iteration continues until the hypothesis is resolved, not until a
difficulty or discriminability threshold is reached.

The ablation conditions used to isolate the Analyzer's contribution are
described in Section~\ref{sec:settings}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

Our experiments ask a single question: \emph{when \textsc{EvalClaw} is given an
evaluation demand, does it produce a benchmark that is valid, informative, and
preferable to what existing pipelines produce on the same demand?} The framework
is treated throughout as a black box that maps a demand $d$ to a benchmark
$\mathcal{B}_d$; we never evaluate the framework on the internal quality of its
intermediate artefacts, only on the benchmark that comes out. This framing forces
two decisions: which demands to feed in, and what ``good benchmark'' means
operationally.

\subsection{Evaluation Settings}
\label{sec:settings}

We instantiate the framework on two demand collections, chosen so that together
they separate the framework's two sources of advantage.

\paragraph{Setting~1: demands beyond existing coverage.}
The 15 capability domains derived in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation-A} are stated
as first-person evaluation demands, each backed by three or more independent
authoritative sources, and each targeting a capability that is either absent from
existing evaluation suites or represented only in isolated domain-specific
systems. Satisfying these demands requires the component-complex, agentic items
described in Section~\ref{sec:harness}: environment configuration, synthetic data,
multi-agent roles, and programmatic evaluation. There is no existing benchmark
for these domains, so the question is not whether \textsc{EvalClaw} beats a
prior pipeline but whether a usable benchmark can be constructed at all, and
whether the resulting items actually probe the intended capability rather than
something adjacent to it.

\paragraph{Setting~2: traditional demands with established references.}
The 10 occupational domains derived in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation-B} correspond
to disciplines---computing, mathematics, law, medicine, the humanities---for which
high-quality human-authored benchmarks already exist. The items these demands
require are predominantly static: multiple choice, short answer, and short
generation, exercising far fewer TaskDefinition components than Setting~1. Here
the framework is not filling a vacuum but competing on ground that is already
occupied, and the appropriate test is whether the benchmark it constructs is
\emph{isomorphic in quality} to the human-authored reference for the same domain:
comparable validity, comparable discrimination, and comparable ordering fidelity,
at a fraction of the construction cost. A framework that only works on novel
demands would fail this setting, and one that only reproduces conventional items
would fail Setting~1; passing both is the claim we make.

\paragraph{Reference benchmarks and construction conditions.}
For each Setting~2 demand we compare against the human-authored benchmark that
currently serves that domain. Because no such reference exists for Setting~1, we
additionally instantiate two paradigm-fixed automated pipelines---document-to-
question transformation and repository-mutation repair-task generation---on every
Setting~1 demand and both Setting~2 domains they can express, giving a
like-for-like automated comparison in both settings.

The ablation conditions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:design} are evaluated on
both settings. For the Planner we compare \textbf{Full}, \textbf{Fixed-source},
and \textbf{No-resource}; for the Harness we compare \textbf{Full Harness} and
\textbf{Harness-free}; for the Analyzer we compare \textbf{Hypothesis-driven} and
\textbf{Error-cluster}. Unless noted, all results use the full pipeline.

\subsection{How We Measure Benchmark Quality}
\label{sec:quality}

Evaluating a constructed benchmark is not the same as evaluating a model on it.
A benchmark can be hard but invalid, discriminative but redundant, or faithful to
its demand but trivially easy. We therefore measure quality at three levels, and
report every metric against a reference: the human-authored benchmark for
Setting~2, and a held-out human sample for Setting~1.

\paragraph{Level 1: item validity.}
The Quality Check gate already guarantees structural well-formedness and
environment executability---items that fail it never enter the benchmark---so we
do not re-measure those properties. We instead audit the three properties that a
schema check cannot see, using a judge with a ternary label
(\emph{pass}~/ \emph{minor defect}~/ \emph{fatal defect}) rather than a Likert
scale, because human annotators agree reliably on fatal defects and barely at all
on fine-grained quality gradations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clarity}: the task statement is unambiguous, and the expected
output format is determinable from the statement alone.
\item \textbf{Correctness}: the reference solution is correct, and the
programmatic evaluator accepts it. This is verified by execution, not by
inspection.
\item \textbf{Faithfulness}: the capability the item exercises is the capability
the demand asked for. This is the property that a paradigm-fixed pipeline is
most likely to violate, since it must convert whatever its corpus contains
regardless of fit.
\end{itemize}

Because the judge is itself a model, its labels are audited against human
annotation on a stratified sample of items drawn from both settings, and we report
Cohen's $\kappa$ for each criterion rather than the judge's accuracy alone. In
addition we include, for every benchmark, a set of \emph{negative controls}:
items that no capable model should be able to solve because the demanded
information is absent, and items a capable model should solve trivially. A
benchmark that scores above chance on the former, or below ceiling on the latter,
is measuring something other than the intended capability.

\paragraph{Level 2: whole-benchmark statistics.}
A benchmark is a measurement instrument, and its value lies in how it distributes
models. We report:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Difficulty distribution}: the distribution of per-item solve rates
across the evaluated model set, reported as the fraction of items in each
difficulty band. A benchmark concentrated at either extreme cannot order models.
\item \textbf{Discrimination}: for each item, the point-biserial correlation
between item score and total score, and the distribution of the IRT
discrimination parameter $a$ across items. We report the mean and the fraction of
items with $a$ indistinguishable from zero, which is the direct measure of how
much of the benchmark is dead weight.
\item \textbf{Separation}: the score gap between the strongest and weakest
evaluated models, expressed as a fraction of the scale, following the
separation metric used in recent adversarially curated benchmarks. This is the
quantity that determines whether the benchmark can still distinguish models
after frontier performance rises.
\item \textbf{Ordering fidelity}: the rank correlation between benchmark score
and independently established model strength. For Setting~2 we use the
human-authored reference's own ranking as the ground truth and report Kendall's
$\tau$ between the two orderings; for Setting~1, where no reference ranking
exists, we use the open-weight model families for which size is a known proxy for
capability and report the correlation between score and parameter count within
each family.
\item \textbf{Retest reliability}: the agreement between two benchmarks
constructed independently from the same demand, measured as the correlation of
per-model scores. This bounds how much of any observed ordering is construction
noise.
\item \textbf{Contamination}: $n$-gram overlap and MinHash near-duplicate rate
between the constructed items and the training corpora of the evaluated models,
reported against the same statistics for the human-authored reference.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Level 3: attribution.}
The first two levels establish that the benchmark is sound. The third asks
whether it is \emph{diagnostic}---whether a low score can be traced to the
capability the demand named. For each Setting~1 demand we construct a
\emph{capability-isolating pair}: two item sets that share task surface, format,
and difficulty but differ in exactly one capability component, one of which is
the component the demand specifies. A benchmark that measures its target
capability should show a significantly larger score drop on the matched set than
on its control. Where a demand's capability can be decomposed into $|D|$
components, we additionally run a per-component ablation and compute attribution
accuracy, the ratio of the target component's contribution to the summed
contribution of all components; an uninformative benchmark yields
$1/|D|$, and we report the ratio against that baseline.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Because the framework's premise is that automated construction is cheaper than
human authoring, we report wall-clock time, token cost, and human-hours for each
demand, on the same per-item basis as the reference benchmark where one exists.

\subsection{Evaluating the Analyzer}
\label{sec:analyzer-eval}

The Analyzer is not a benchmark constructor but a diagnostic procedure, and
Level~3 metrics above only establish that \emph{a} signal exists, not that the
Analyzer's account of it is correct. We therefore evaluate it separately, on a
different question: given a model's failures on an existing pool, does the
Analyzer produce a diagnosis that is systematic and true?

\paragraph{Systematicness.}
The failure of proxy-metric iteration is that its items cluster only by surface
similarity. We measure whether the Analyzer's output clusters by
\emph{capability}: the items generated within a single hypothesis round should be
tighter in the capability component they exercise than a random sample of the
pool, while items generated across rounds should be maximally separated in that
same space. Concretely, we compute the ratio of within-round to between-round
variance in capability-component embeddings, and compare against the
Error-cluster ablation, which we expect to show the opposite pattern: tight
surface clustering with no capability structure. We additionally check coverage:
across rounds, does the Analyzer's hypothesis sequence span the capability
dimensions of the pool, or does it revisit one region of the space? We report the
number of distinct capability dimensions diagnosed before the first repeat, and
the balance of the resulting item pool, against the pool produced by
Error-cluster.

\paragraph{Credibility.}
Every hypothesis is accompanied by a verification round, so a diagnosis is
credible exactly when the verification round was both run and decisive. We
report, per diagnosis: whether the hypothesis was confirmed or refuted, and on
what evidence; whether the confirmation rests on the targeted items alone or
survives comparison against the capability-isolating controls of
Section~\ref{sec:quality}, which rules out the trivial case where the new items
are merely harder; and whether the diagnostic report states a capability gap that
is falsifiable, in the sense that we can construct a counterexample item the
model passes. We score this with the same ternary judge and human audit used for
Level~1, and we measure the Analyzer's \emph{precision} as the fraction of
confirmed hypotheses that survive an independent re-test on newly constructed
items, against Error-cluster run for the same number of rounds and the same
token budget.

\paragraph{Headline comparison.}
The three components are evaluated jointly on one endpoint: for each Setting~1
demand and each Setting~2 domain, we report the benchmark-level quality profile
(Levels 1--3) produced by the full pipeline and by each ablation, so that every
component's contribution is read off the same instrument rather than from a
component-specific proxy.

\section{Derivation of Capability Domains from Authoritative Sources}\label{app:derivation-A}

We derive our 15 capability domains from 14 authoritative sources spanning regulatory frameworks, evaluation methodologies, and empirical benchmarks. Sources S1--S12 provide prescriptive requirements established through expert consensus, regulatory processes, or peer review. Sources S13--S14 provide empirical evidence of capability gaps, based on reproducible evaluation results published in early 2026, after the knowledge cutoff of mainstream foundation models.

\subsection{Atomic Requirement Extraction}

We extracted prescriptive statements from S1--S14 and decomposed them into atomic requirements. Key extractions include:

\paragraph{From S1 (NIST GenAI Profile):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{CEA} (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, Nuclear, Explosives) $\rightarrow$ ``detect covert behavior under privilege escalation''
\item \textit{Environmental impacts} $\rightarrow$ ``resource budget control for tokens, latency, and compute''
\item \textit{Human-AI configuration} $\rightarrow$ ``detect and adapt to silent dependency loss'' + ``resolve conflicts across multiple legitimate principals with a principled priority ordering'' + ``restrict actions to the minimal footprint required by the task''
\item \textit{Information integrity} $\rightarrow$ ``identify and trace synthetic content''
\item \textit{Information security} $\rightarrow$ ``defend against prompt injection and malicious content''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{From S3--S4 (UK AISI):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Autonomous systems requirements} $\rightarrow$ ``sustain multi-step tasks under no supervision'' + ``resume from saved state after interruptions'' + ``operate professional software via GUI/CLI'' + ``detect and adapt to silent dependency loss mid-task''
\item \textit{Misuse track} $\rightarrow$ ``execute multi-step cyber attack/defense chains''
\item \textit{Standards} $\rightarrow$ ``cross-session state persistence'' + ``resolve multi-principal instruction conflicts with a principled priority ordering'' + ``restrict task footprint to the minimum required scope''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{From S6--S7 (METR):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Task-standard format} $\rightarrow$ ``checkpoint mechanisms for long-horizon tasks'' + ``cross-session memory''
\item \textit{Task suites} $\rightarrow$ ``mixed operations in real software'' + ``infer undocumented project conventions from surrounding artifacts'' + ``end-to-end cybersecurity execution''
\item \textit{Elicitation protocols} $\rightarrow$ ``interrupt-resume path testing'' + ``multi-agent orchestration with delegation tracking and failure attribution'' + ``detect and adapt to silent dependency loss'' + ``minimal footprint and permission scoping''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{From S8 (DARPA):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{DICE program} $\rightarrow$ ``decentralized multi-agent long-horizon coordination with delegation tracking and failure attribution'' + ``adversarial robustness'' + ``exploration and loss-cutting in uncertain environments''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{From S9--S10 (OECD):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Creative Thinking Framework} $\rightarrow$ ``creativity assessment of synthetic content''
\item \textit{Learning Compass} $\rightarrow$ ``identify own knowledge boundaries'' + ``infer undocumented project conventions from surrounding artifacts''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{From S12 (ISO/IEC 42001):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Capability requirements} $\rightarrow$ ``compute and latency budgets''
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{From S13--S14 (empirical gaps):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{ARC-AGI-3} $\rightarrow$ ``goal inference and mechanism discovery in no-instruction environments''
\item \textit{Terminal-Bench Science} $\rightarrow$ ``hypothesis formation with penalties for incorrect hypotheses''
\end{itemize}

Clusters supported by three or more sources were prioritized. \subsection{Excluded Requirements}

The following requirements were excluded due to technical or ethical constraints:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S1 obscene/dangerous content generation:} Reformulated to test refusal rates instead of generation capability
\item \textbf{S1 intellectual property and value chain:} Require cross-organizational supply chain data unavailable in sandbox environments
\item \textbf{S3 societal impacts (long-term components):} Time scales exceed benchmark feasibility
\item \textbf{S10 reconciling tensions (real stakeholder trade-offs):} Sandbox environments cannot construct decidable outcomes for actual organizational decisions
\item \textbf{Model self-improvement and self-replication (METR AI R\&D task family):} Tested only under restricted-access safety evaluation frameworks, not in public benchmarks
\item \textbf{Representation fairness audits involving real demographic attribute inference:} Such audits themselves constitute privacy risks
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Derivation}

We select the SOC major groups whose work is primarily knowledge work
(deliverables are digital artifacts or decisions are
information-system-mediated), which covers management, business and finance,
computing, engineering, sciences, law, education, arts and media, healthcare,
and administrative support.
Trades, transport, personal services, and similar groups whose core
deliverables are physical are excluded.

Cross-checking the retained groups against ISCED-F~2013 reveals two gaps.
SOC covers art and design practitioners (group~27) but has almost no
occupations corresponding to historians, philosophers, or linguists; and
agricultural science, veterinary science, and food science are entirely absent
because the farming group was excluded.
Both are added via the ISCED-F anchor.
